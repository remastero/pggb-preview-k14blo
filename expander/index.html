PGGB Expander Plots

The Dynamic Range Expander rebuilds the tops of waveforms that were clipped flat when a master was made too loud. Where a track was squeezed by a limiter, it can also give back some of the peaks the limiter took. These plots show what it does to 24 tracks, at the track's own sample rate and after PGGB upsamples it to 16fS.

Reading a plot

Grey: the originalThe track as it is.
Orange: expandedThe same track through the Expander. Wherever orange shows, the Expander changed the waveform.
Red dashes: full scale0 dBFS, the highest level a track can store.

Each picture shows the left and right channels: first the whole track, then a close-up of the moment the Expander changed most. The 16fS pictures show both versions upsampled by PGGB's Precision Optimized Engine, put back on the original's level, with the original's own samples as dots.

How the Expander decides

Declip

  • It doesn't look for full scale. It looks at the highest level each channel reaches, for positive and negative peaks separately. A master clipped and then turned down is still found.
  • A clip level is a pile-up of samples on a track's top value, many times more than on the values just below it.
  • A clipped run is at least 3 samples on that level, cut off steeply at both ends. Natural peaks round off; clipped ones drop away.
  • Clipping is confirmed only when at least 5 runs pass that test, and they make up at least half of that level's runs.
  • The repair rebuilds only the clipped samples, never more than 6 dB above the clip level. Every other sample stays exactly as it was.

De-limit

  • Only when a track shows all three signs of a limiter:
  • Crowded at the top: at least 0.3 % of samples within 1 dB of the track's ceiling.
  • On transients: most of those samples are drum hits and attacks, not a sustained loud note.
  • Squeezed: even the track's least dynamic stretches have a peak-to-average range under 9.5 dB.
  • It steps aside on a track that declip repaired: there, clipping alone can look like a limiter, and lifting it would do harm.
  • On DSD recordings it works after PGGB converts them to PCM. Declip has nothing to find there.
  • Always de-limit is an option for comparing: it turns the de-limiter on for every track, whatever its signs say.
  • The lift is at most 3 dB, easing in near the ceiling, then easing off after each hit.

Clipped: the Metallica album, flat at 0 dBFS on every track, and three tracks each from Rush's Clockwork Angels and Adele's 21. The Expander repairs them with declip, changing under 1 % of their samples.

Limited: the tracks by David Bowie, Foo Fighters, Rihanna and Bruno Mars show all three signs of a limiter, so the Expander de-limits them on its own. Most of their samples change, because the lift of at most 3 dB rises and falls with the music.

The Rush, Adele and limited tracks were picked from 227 loud tracks for how much the Expander raised their DR.

Not limited: Texas Flood and Bright Mississippi, so the Expander leaves them exactly as they are. To show what the de-limiter does, their "expanded" plots have it forced on.

The Metallica album appears twice: as the Expander leaves it (declip only), and with Always de-limit, the option for comparing, where the de-limiter is forced on as well.

DR is the usual dynamic range value, as a DR meter reads it: the peaks against the loudest passages' average. Higher means more dynamic. The tables show it for the original and for the expanded version.
